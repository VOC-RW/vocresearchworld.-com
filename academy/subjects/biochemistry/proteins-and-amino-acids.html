02 · INTRODUCTION

What are proteins?

Proteins are complex biological macromolecules composed of amino acid residues joined together through peptide bonds. They contain carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. Many proteins also contain sulphur, while certain proteins contain additional elements such as phosphorus or metals.

Proteins are essential components of all living cells. They participate in almost every major biological process, including catalysis, transport, movement, immune defence, cellular communication, structural support and regulation of gene expression.

Examples include haemoglobin, collagen, insulin, albumin, antibodies, actin, myosin and numerous enzymes.

Fundamental concept: The biological activity of a protein depends strongly on its three-dimensional structure. A change in its amino acid sequence or folding pattern can affect its stability and function.

Why proteins are important in biochemistry

Proteins are not simply nutrients for growth and tissue repair. They are functional molecules that control biochemical reactions, maintain cellular organisation and allow organisms to respond to their environments.

Enzymes accelerate metabolic reactions. Haemoglobin transports oxygen. Antibodies recognise foreign substances. Collagen provides mechanical strength to connective tissues. Membrane proteins facilitate transport of substances across biological membranes.

03 · AMINO ACID CHEMISTRY

Structure of amino acids

Amino acids are organic compounds containing an amino group and a carboxyl group. They are the fundamental building blocks from which proteins are constructed.

Most amino acids incorporated into proteins have a central carbon atom called the alpha carbon (Cα). It is attached to four groups:

  • An amino group (–NH₂ or –NH₃⁺).
  • A carboxyl group (–COOH or –COO⁻).
  • A hydrogen atom (–H).
  • A variable side chain represented by R.

The R group determines the identity and chemical properties of each amino acid.

The alpha carbon

The alpha carbon is the central carbon atom to which the amino group, carboxyl group, hydrogen and side chain are attached. In most amino acids it is asymmetric, producing stereoisomerism.

Glycine is the exception because its side chain is another hydrogen atom. Its alpha carbon therefore has two identical substituents and is not chiral.

Zwitterionic nature of amino acids

In aqueous solutions near physiological pH, amino acids commonly exist as zwitterions. In this form, the amino group is protonated (–NH₃⁺) while the carboxyl group is deprotonated (–COO⁻).

⁺H₃N–CH(R)–COO⁻

A zwitterion has both a positive and a negative charge, but its overall net charge may be zero.

Isoelectric point (pI)

The isoelectric point is the pH at which an amino acid or protein has zero net electrical charge. At this pH, its positive and negative charges balance.

The isoelectric point is important in electrophoresis, protein purification and separation techniques.

04 · CLASSIFICATION

Classification of amino acids

Amino acids can be classified according to the chemical nature of their side chains, nutritional requirements and metabolic fate.

B. Classification according to nutritional requirements

Essential amino acids cannot be synthesised by the human body in sufficient quantities to meet normal requirements. They must therefore be obtained through the diet.

Essential amino acids

  • Histidine
  • Isoleucine
  • Leucine
  • Lysine
  • Methionine
  • Phenylalanine
  • Threonine
  • Tryptophan
  • Valine

Non-essential amino acids

These can generally be synthesised by the body under normal physiological conditions. Examples include alanine, aspartate, glutamate and serine.

Conditionally essential amino acids

Certain amino acids may become conditionally essential during periods of rapid growth, illness, trauma or metabolic stress because the body's capacity to synthesise them may not meet demand.

Examples include arginine, cysteine, glutamine, glycine, proline and tyrosine in particular physiological circumstances.

Important distinction: Essential does not mean that an amino acid is more important biologically. It means that the body cannot produce enough of it under normal conditions, making dietary intake necessary.

C. Classification according to metabolic fate

  • Glucogenic amino acids: Their carbon skeletons can contribute to glucose production through gluconeogenesis.
  • Ketogenic amino acids: Their carbon skeletons produce acetyl-CoA or acetoacetate-related products that can contribute to ketone body formation.
  • Both glucogenic and ketogenic: Their breakdown products can enter both metabolic pathways.

Leucine and lysine are exclusively ketogenic. Isoleucine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and tyrosine are both glucogenic and ketogenic. The remaining standard amino acids are glucogenic.

05 · PEPTIDE BOND FORMATION

How amino acids form peptides and proteins

Amino acids are joined together through peptide bonds. A peptide bond is a covalent amide linkage formed between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of another.

In a simplified chemical representation, bond formation involves removal of the elements of water. In living cells, protein synthesis occurs through the ribosome using activated aminoacyl-tRNA molecules.

Amino acid + Amino acid → Dipeptide + H₂O

Polypeptides

When many amino acids are linked by peptide bonds, a polypeptide chain is produced. One or more polypeptide chains may fold and associate to form a functional protein.

Direction of a polypeptide chain

A polypeptide has two distinct ends:

  • N-terminus: The end with a free amino group.
  • C-terminus: The end with a free carboxyl group.

Protein sequences are conventionally written from the N-terminus to the C-terminus.

Properties of the peptide bond

  • It has partial double-bond character due to resonance.
  • It is relatively rigid and planar.
  • Rotation around the peptide C–N bond is restricted.
  • It is generally found in the trans configuration.
  • It contributes to the organisation of protein structure.

Biochemical significance: The amino acid sequence determines the chemical possibilities available to a protein during folding. This sequence is therefore central to protein structure and function.

06 · PROTEIN CLASSIFICATION

Classification of proteins

A. According to shape

Fibrous proteins

Fibrous proteins usually have elongated structures and are often associated with mechanical support, strength and protection. They are commonly less soluble in water than globular proteins.

Examples include collagen, keratin and elastin.

Globular proteins

Globular proteins generally have compact, folded structures. Many are soluble in aqueous environments and perform dynamic biological functions.

Examples include haemoglobin, albumin, insulin and many enzymes.

B. According to chemical composition

ClassDescriptionExamples
Simple proteinsYield mainly amino acids on hydrolysisAlbumins, globulins, histones
Conjugated proteinsContain a protein component and a non-protein componentHaemoglobin, glycoproteins, lipoproteins
Derived proteinsProduced through physical or chemical modification of proteinsProteoses, peptones and certain denatured proteins

Conjugated proteins and their prosthetic groups

A conjugated protein contains a non-protein component associated with its protein portion. This non-protein component is often called a prosthetic group when it is tightly associated with the protein.

  • Glycoproteins: Proteins associated with carbohydrate groups.
  • Lipoproteins: Complexes of proteins and lipids.
  • Metalloproteins: Proteins containing metal ions.
  • Chromoproteins: Proteins associated with coloured groups, such as haem-containing proteins.
  • Phosphoproteins: Proteins containing covalently attached phosphate groups.

07 · STRUCTURAL ORGANISATION

The four levels of protein structure

Protein structure is commonly described at four levels: primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary. These levels explain how a linear amino acid sequence becomes an organised biological molecule.

1. Primary structure

Primary structure refers to the specific linear sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain. The amino acids are connected through peptide bonds.

Even a single amino acid substitution may affect protein properties and biological function. For example, sickle cell disease is associated with a substitution of glutamic acid by valine in the beta-globin chain of haemoglobin.

2. Secondary structure

Secondary structure refers to local, regular folding patterns of the polypeptide backbone. The major examples are the alpha helix and beta-pleated sheet.

Alpha helix:

An alpha helix is a coiled structure stabilised mainly by hydrogen bonds between the carbonyl oxygen of one peptide bond and the N–H group of another peptide bond further along the chain.

Beta-pleated sheet:

A beta sheet consists of extended polypeptide segments arranged side by side. Hydrogen bonds between backbone groups stabilise the structure. The strands may be parallel or antiparallel.

3. Tertiary structure

Tertiary structure describes the overall three-dimensional arrangement of a single polypeptide chain.

It is stabilised by several interactions:

  • Hydrophobic interactions between non-polar side chains.
  • Hydrogen bonds.
  • Ionic interactions and salt bridges.
  • Van der Waals interactions.
  • Disulphide bonds between cysteine residues.

4. Quaternary structure

Quaternary structure occurs when two or more polypeptide subunits associate to form a functional protein complex.

Haemoglobin is a familiar example. Adult haemoglobin A normally consists of two alpha and two beta polypeptide chains (α₂β₂).

Simplified conceptual illustration of the four levels of protein structure.

Examination guide: Primary structure describes sequence; secondary structure describes local folding; tertiary structure describes the three-dimensional form of one chain; quaternary structure describes the association of multiple polypeptide chains.

08 · PROTEIN FOLDING

Protein folding and denaturation

Protein folding

Protein folding is the process through which a polypeptide chain adopts a biologically functional three-dimensional conformation.

Folding is influenced by the amino acid sequence, interactions between side chains, the surrounding chemical environment and, in many cases, molecular chaperones.

Molecular chaperones

Molecular chaperones are proteins that assist other proteins in achieving or maintaining appropriate conformations. They can help prevent inappropriate aggregation and assist protein recovery under cellular stress.

Protein denaturation

Denaturation is the disruption of the native three-dimensional structure of a protein. It often results in loss of biological activity.

Common denaturing factors include:

  • High temperature.
  • Extreme acidic or alkaline pH.
  • Organic solvents.
  • Detergents.
  • Heavy metal ions.
  • Some chemical agents such as urea.

Effects of denaturation

Denaturation may expose previously buried amino acid residues, reduce solubility, cause aggregation and alter enzyme activity. In some circumstances, denaturation is reversible; in others, it is effectively irreversible.

Practical example: Heating egg white causes albumin proteins to denature and aggregate, changing the transparent liquid into a white solid. This is a familiar example of protein structural change.

09 · BIOLOGICAL FUNCTIONS

Major functions of proteins in living organisms

1. Enzymatic function

Enzymes are biological catalysts. Most enzymes are proteins, although some RNA molecules can also catalyse reactions. Enzymes increase reaction rates by lowering activation energy without being consumed in the overall reaction.

Examples include amylase, pepsin, catalase and DNA polymerase.

2. Structural function

Structural proteins provide support, strength and organisation to tissues and cells. Collagen is abundant in connective tissues, while keratin occurs in hair, nails and the outer layers of skin.

3. Transport function

Haemoglobin transports oxygen in red blood cells. Albumin transports various substances in plasma. Membrane transport proteins facilitate the movement of ions and molecules across cell membranes.

4. Hormonal and regulatory function

Some hormones are proteins or peptides. Insulin regulates blood glucose, while glucagon contributes to glucose homeostasis during fasting.

5. Defence and immune function

Antibodies are immunoglobulin proteins that recognise specific antigens. They participate in immune defence and help the body respond to foreign substances.

6. Movement and contraction

Actin and myosin are proteins involved in muscle contraction. Their interaction, supported by ATP, produces force and movement.

7. Storage function

Certain proteins store important substances. Ferritin stores iron in cells, while casein contributes to nutrient storage in milk.

8. Energy contribution

Proteins are not the body's preferred primary energy reserve, but amino acids can be oxidised when required. Their carbon skeletons enter metabolic pathways to support energy production.

10 · DIGESTION AND ABSORPTION

Protein digestion in humans

Dietary proteins must be broken down into smaller peptides and amino acids before their absorption through the intestinal epithelium.

Mouth

Mechanical digestion begins in the mouth through chewing. However, significant enzymatic protein digestion does not normally occur in the mouth.

Stomach

Gastric hydrochloric acid creates an acidic environment and helps denature dietary proteins. It also supports the conversion of pepsinogen into the active enzyme pepsin.

Pepsin is an endopeptidase that breaks selected peptide bonds, producing smaller peptides.

Small intestine

The pancreas releases proteolytic enzymes into the small intestine. Many are secreted as inactive precursors called zymogens to reduce the risk of pancreatic tissue damage.

  • Trypsin: Activated from trypsinogen and cleaves peptide bonds mainly after lysine and arginine residues.
  • Chymotrypsin: Acts preferentially near aromatic amino acid residues.
  • Elastase: Cleaves peptide bonds near certain small neutral amino acid residues.
  • Carboxypeptidases: Remove amino acids from the C-terminal end of peptides.
  • Brush-border peptidases: Further digest peptides at the intestinal surface.

Activation of pancreatic proteases

Enteropeptidase, located on the intestinal brush border, activates trypsinogen to trypsin. Trypsin then activates additional pancreatic zymogens. This activation cascade is essential for efficient protein digestion.

Absorption of amino acids

Amino acids are absorbed through specific transport systems in the intestinal epithelium. Small dipeptides and tripeptides can also enter intestinal cells through peptide transport systems and are commonly hydrolysed into amino acids inside the cells.

Absorbed amino acids enter the portal circulation and are transported to the liver and other tissues.

Clinical connection: Disorders affecting pancreatic enzyme production, intestinal function or protein digestion can contribute to malabsorption and nutritional deficiencies.

11 · AMINO ACID METABOLISM

Metabolism of amino acids

Amino acid metabolism involves the synthesis, breakdown and conversion of amino acids and their derivatives. Unlike carbohydrates and lipids, the nitrogen component of amino acids must also be managed during their breakdown.

Transamination

Transamination is the transfer of an amino group from an amino acid to an alpha-keto acid. The reaction is catalysed by aminotransferase enzymes and commonly requires pyridoxal phosphate, a derivative of vitamin B₆.

Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) are important examples.

Amino acid + α-ketoglutarate ⇌ α-keto acid + glutamate

Deamination

Deamination involves the removal of an amino group from a molecule. Oxidative deamination of glutamate, catalysed by glutamate dehydrogenase, can release ammonia.

Ammonia and toxicity

Ammonia is toxic, particularly to the nervous system, when it accumulates in excessive amounts. The body therefore converts much of the nitrogen derived from amino acid breakdown into urea in the liver.

The urea cycle

The urea cycle is a series of biochemical reactions that converts toxic nitrogen into urea, which can be transported in the blood and eliminated by the kidneys.

The cycle occurs through reactions distributed between the mitochondria and cytosol of liver cells. Its major intermediates include carbamoyl phosphate, citrulline, argininosuccinate and arginine.

Nitrogen from amino acid metabolism → Urea → Urine

Carbon skeleton utilisation

After removal or transfer of the amino group, the remaining carbon skeleton can enter metabolic pathways.

It may be used for:

  • Energy production through oxidation.
  • Glucose synthesis through gluconeogenesis.
  • Ketone body production.
  • Synthesis of fatty acids and other biomolecules.

Protein synthesis

Protein synthesis occurs through transcription and translation. During translation, ribosomes read messenger RNA codons and use transfer RNA molecules to deliver the corresponding amino acids in the correct sequence.

Important distinction: Transamination transfers an amino group; deamination removes an amino group. The urea cycle helps eliminate nitrogen safely from the body.

12 · PROTEIN REQUIREMENTS AND NUTRITION

Dietary proteins and nutritional quality

Complete and incomplete protein sources

Protein quality depends partly on the amount and balance of essential amino acids supplied by a food and how effectively the protein is digested and utilised.

Foods such as eggs, milk, fish, meat and certain soy products can provide all essential amino acids in useful proportions.

Many plant foods contain protein but may have relatively low amounts of particular essential amino acids. Combining different plant protein sources over the course of a balanced diet can improve overall amino acid adequacy.

Protein-energy malnutrition

Severe or prolonged inadequate intake of energy and protein can cause protein-energy malnutrition.

  • Kwashiorkor: A severe form of malnutrition associated with oedema and other clinical features. It is not adequately explained by protein deficiency alone; multiple nutritional and physiological factors contribute.
  • Marasmus: Severe energy deficiency associated with marked wasting and loss of body tissue.

Nitrogen balance

Nitrogen balance compares nitrogen intake, mainly from dietary protein, with nitrogen losses from the body.

  • Positive nitrogen balance: Nitrogen intake exceeds nitrogen loss, as may occur during growth, pregnancy or recovery from illness.
  • Negative nitrogen balance: Nitrogen loss exceeds intake, as may occur during starvation, severe illness or inadequate protein intake.
  • Equilibrium: Nitrogen intake and loss are approximately balanced.

13 · LABORATORY BIOCHEMISTRY

Laboratory analysis of proteins and amino acids

Protein analysis is important in clinical biochemistry, food science, pharmaceutical research, biotechnology and laboratory quality control. Both qualitative and quantitative methods are used.

Biuret test

The Biuret test detects peptide bonds. Under alkaline conditions, copper(II) ions form a violet-coloured complex with compounds containing suitable peptide bonds.

A violet or purple colour is a positive indication of peptide-containing substances. Free amino acids generally do not give the same positive Biuret response.

Ninhydrin test

Ninhydrin reacts with many free amino groups, producing a characteristic coloured product. Most alpha amino acids produce a purple-blue colour known as Ruhemann's purple.

Proline and hydroxyproline, which contain secondary amino groups, commonly produce a yellowish response.

Xanthoproteic test

This test detects certain aromatic amino acid residues in proteins. Concentrated nitric acid reacts with aromatic rings, producing yellow nitrated products.

Millon's test

Millon's test is traditionally associated with the phenolic group of tyrosine. A positive reaction can produce a red colour after appropriate treatment and heating.

Sulphur test

Certain chemical procedures can detect sulphur-containing amino acid residues, particularly those derived from cysteine and cystine.

Quantitative protein determination

MethodPrincipleApplication

Bradford assayBinding of Coomassie dye to proteinsProtein concentration measurement

BCA assayProtein-dependent reduction of copper followed by colour formationProtein quantification

Electrophoresis

Electrophoresis separates charged molecules under an applied electric field. Protein movement depends on properties such as molecular size, shape and net charge.

Serum protein electrophoresis can separate major protein fractions, including albumin and globulin fractions. It is useful in investigating certain clinical conditions.

SDS-PAGE

Sodium dodecyl sulphate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis is commonly used to separate proteins primarily according to molecular mass under denaturing conditions.

Chromatography

Protein purification can involve ion-exchange, size-exclusion and affinity chromatography. These techniques separate proteins according to differences in charge, size or specific binding interactions.

Spectrophotometry

Many proteins absorb ultraviolet light, particularly around 280 nm, largely because of aromatic amino acid residues such as tryptophan and tyrosine. This property can be used for protein concentration estimation when appropriate conditions and corrections are applied.

Laboratory safety: Protein tests may involve corrosive acids, alkalis, metal salts and heating. Use appropriate personal protective equipment, consult safety data sheets and follow validated laboratory procedures.

14 · CLINICAL BIOCHEMISTRY

Clinical importance of proteins

Serum albumin

Albumin is the most abundant plasma protein. It helps maintain colloid osmotic pressure and transports various substances, including fatty acids, hormones and certain drugs.

Reduced albumin concentration may occur in several conditions, including liver dysfunction, kidney protein loss, inflammation and inadequate nutritional intake. Interpretation requires clinical context.

Globulins

Globulins include several groups of proteins involved in immunity, transport and other biological processes. Immunoglobulins are important components of the adaptive immune system.

Enzymes as clinical markers

Some enzymes are measured in blood to assist the investigation of tissue injury or disease.

  • ALT: Commonly used in the assessment of liver cell injury.
  • AST: Present in several tissues and interpreted alongside other findings.
  • Creatine kinase: May help investigate muscle injury.
  • Amylase and lipase: May be used in evaluating pancreatic disorders.

Sickle cell disease

Sickle cell disease is associated with abnormal haemoglobin, particularly haemoglobin S. A specific amino acid substitution in the beta-globin chain changes haemoglobin behaviour under low-oxygen conditions, encouraging polymer formation and distortion of red blood cells.

Inborn errors of amino acid metabolism

Certain inherited disorders affect enzymes involved in amino acid metabolism. An example is phenylketonuria (PKU), which is commonly associated with impaired phenylalanine hydroxylase activity or related defects in phenylalanine metabolism.

Early diagnosis and appropriate dietary management are important in reducing complications.

15 · RESEARCH AND INDUSTRIAL APPLICATIONS

Applications of proteins in science and industry

Biotechnology

Recombinant DNA technology allows scientists to produce selected proteins using genetically modified microorganisms or other expression systems. Insulin and several therapeutic proteins are produced using biotechnology.

Pharmaceutical science

Therapeutic proteins include certain hormones, antibodies, enzymes and blood-related biological products. Their development requires careful control of purity, stability, biological activity and manufacturing quality.

Food science

Proteins influence food texture, water retention, emulsification, gel formation and nutritional value. Protein denaturation and aggregation are important in cooking and food processing.

Analytical research

Proteomics is the large-scale study of proteins, including their abundance, structure, interactions and modifications. Mass spectrometry and advanced separation techniques are widely used in proteomic investigations.

Artificial intelligence and protein science

Computational methods and artificial intelligence are increasingly used to predict protein structures, study molecular interactions and support protein engineering. Experimental validation remains important when interpreting computational predictions.

16 · CHAPTER SUMMARY

Key points to remember

  • Proteins are biological macromolecules made from amino acid residues.
  • A typical alpha amino acid contains an amino group, carboxyl group, hydrogen and variable R group.
  • Amino acids can be classified by side-chain properties, nutritional requirements and metabolic fate.
  • Essential amino acids must be obtained through the diet in adequate amounts.
  • Peptide bonds connect amino acids to form polypeptide chains.
  • Protein structure has four levels: primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary.
  • Protein folding produces functional three-dimensional structures.
  • Denaturation can disrupt protein structure and biological activity.
  • Proteins perform enzymatic, structural, transport, defence and regulatory functions.
  • Protein digestion involves gastric and pancreatic proteases as well as intestinal peptidases.
  • Amino acid metabolism involves transamination, deamination and nitrogen disposal through the urea cycle.
  • Laboratory protein analysis includes Biuret, Bradford, Lowry, electrophoresis and chromatography methods.
