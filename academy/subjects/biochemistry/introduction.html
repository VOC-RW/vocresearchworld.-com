LIFE AND HEALTH SCIENCES · BIOCHEMISTRY

Topic 01: Introduction to Biochemistry

Discover the chemistry of life. Understand the molecules, chemical reactions and biological processes responsible for the structure, activities and survival of living organisms.

Learning level: Beginner to University Foundation

WELCOME TO YOUR LESSON

Before we begin

Imagine looking at a human being, a plant, a microorganism or even a single living cell. Each is made up of chemical substances that interact continuously to maintain life. These substances include water, carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, vitamins and minerals.

But how does food become energy? How does the body build new tissues? How do enzymes accelerate chemical reactions? How does DNA store information that helps determine biological characteristics?

These are some of the questions that biochemistry helps us understand.

02 · FUNDAMENTAL CONCEPT

What is Biochemistry?

Biochemistry is the branch of science concerned with the chemical composition, molecular structure, properties and chemical reactions of substances found in living organisms.

In simpler terms, biochemistry explains life at the molecular and chemical levels. It investigates what living organisms are made of and how their chemical components work together to sustain life.

Scientific definition: Biochemistry is the study of the chemical substances and processes occurring in living organisms, including the molecular mechanisms underlying biological functions.

Why is it called Biochemistry?

The word combines two major scientific ideas:

  • Bio: Relating to life or living organisms.
  • Chemistry: The science of matter, its composition, properties and transformations.

Therefore, biochemistry applies chemical principles to the understanding of living systems.

03 · SCIENTIFIC FOUNDATION

Relationship between Biology and Chemistry

Biology studies living organisms, their structures, functions, growth, reproduction and interactions. Chemistry studies matter, chemical composition, molecular structures and reactions.

Biochemistry connects these disciplines by explaining the chemical mechanisms responsible for biological activities.

Conceptual relationship between biology and chemistry.

Example: Digestion of food

When a person eats rice, the starch present in the rice is broken down into smaller carbohydrate molecules by digestive enzymes.

These molecules can subsequently be absorbed and used in cellular metabolism to generate energy.

Biology explains the digestive system and its functions. Chemistry explains the molecular structures and reactions. Biochemistry explains the chemical processes that make digestion and energy utilisation possible.

04 · CHEMICAL COMPOSITION OF LIFE

Major biomolecules

Living organisms contain different chemical substances that perform specific biological functions. Many of the important organic molecules are collectively called biomolecules.

Biomolecule Major function Example
Carbohydrates Energy supply and structural support Glucose, starch
Proteins Structure, transport and catalysis Haemoglobin, enzymes
Lipids Energy storage and cell membranes Triglycerides, phospholipids
Nucleic acids Storage and expression of genetic information DNA, RNA

Other important chemical substances

  • Water: A major biological solvent and medium for many biochemical reactions.
  • Minerals: Inorganic elements that support biological processes, such as iron, calcium and zinc.
  • Vitamins: Organic micronutrients required for normal physiological and metabolic functions.

05 · CELLULAR BIOCHEMISTRY

Biochemistry and the living cell

The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life. Many biochemical reactions occur inside cells, often within specialised compartments called organelles.

Examples of important cellular structures

  • Nucleus: Contains most of the cell's genetic material in eukaryotic cells.
  • Mitochondria: Major sites of ATP production through aerobic respiration in many eukaryotic cells.
  • Ribosomes: Sites of protein synthesis.
  • Cytoplasm: Contains cellular components and is the site of many metabolic reactions.
  • Cell membrane: Regulates movement of substances into and out of the cell.

Remember: Biochemistry does not study only the human body. It also investigates chemical processes in plants, animals, microorganisms and other living systems.

06 · BRANCHES AND APPLICATIONS

Major areas of biochemistry

Structural Biochemistry

Studies the structures and molecular organisation of biological substances.

Metabolic Biochemistry

Studies chemical pathways involved in energy production and biosynthesis.

Enzymology

Investigates enzymes, their mechanisms, activities and regulation.

Molecular Biology

Examines DNA, RNA, gene expression and molecular mechanisms of inheritance.

Clinical Biochemistry

Uses biochemical investigations to support the assessment and monitoring of health conditions.

Plant Biochemistry

Studies photosynthesis, plant metabolism and the chemistry of plant products.

Practical applications

Biochemistry contributes to many areas of modern science and society.

  • Medicine: Understanding disease mechanisms and biochemical laboratory markers.
  • Pharmaceutical science: Supporting drug discovery and understanding drug action.
  • Agriculture: Investigating plant growth, crop quality and nutrient metabolism.
  • Food science: Understanding food composition, preservation and nutritional quality.
  • Biotechnology: Applying biological molecules and processes in industrial and scientific production.
  • Research: Investigating molecular mechanisms, biological pathways and new scientific discoveries.

07 · BIOCHEMISTRY IN THE LABORATORY

Connection with laboratory science

Biochemistry is strongly connected with laboratory investigations. Scientists use analytical methods to identify, measure and study biological substances.

Examples of laboratory methods

  • Spectrophotometry: Measures light absorption to help determine the concentration of substances.
  • Chromatography: Separates components of mixtures.
  • Electrophoresis: Separates charged molecules using an electric field.
  • Colorimetric methods: Estimate the concentration of substances through colour changes.
  • Enzyme assays: Measure enzyme activity under defined experimental conditions.

Laboratory example: A clinical laboratory may measure glucose concentration in a blood sample using an enzymatic assay. The result can help healthcare professionals assess glucose regulation in the appropriate clinical context.

08 · LESSON SUMMARY

What you should remember

  • Biochemistry studies the chemical basis of life.
  • It connects the principles of biology and chemistry.
  • Major biomolecules include carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and nucleic acids.
  • Biochemical reactions occur throughout living cells.
  • Enzymes help accelerate many biological chemical reactions.
  • Biochemistry has applications in medicine, agriculture, food science, biotechnology and laboratory research.

09 · REVISION QUESTIONS

Test your understanding

1. Define biochemistry.

Biochemistry is the branch of science that studies the chemical substances, molecular structures and chemical processes occurring in living organisms.

2. Explain the relationship between biology, chemistry and biochemistry.

Biology studies living organisms and their functions. Chemistry studies matter and its transformations. Biochemistry combines these principles to explain the chemical mechanisms underlying biological processes.

3. List four major biomolecules.

Carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and nucleic acids.

4. State five applications of biochemistry.

Medicine, pharmaceutical science, agriculture, food science and biotechnology.

5. Explain the importance of biochemistry in laboratory science.

It provides the scientific basis for laboratory investigations involving biological substances, including glucose, enzymes, proteins and other biochemical markers.
