02 · INTRODUCTION

What is enzymology?

Enzymology is the branch of biochemistry concerned with the study of enzymes, including their structure, properties, catalytic mechanisms, kinetics, regulation and biological functions.

Enzymes are biological catalysts that increase the rate of biochemical reactions without being consumed permanently in the reactions they catalyse. Most enzymes are proteins, although certain RNA molecules also possess catalytic activity.

Enzymes are essential because many reactions necessary for life would proceed too slowly under normal cellular conditions without catalytic assistance. They allow metabolism, digestion, energy production, DNA replication and numerous other processes to occur at biologically useful rates.

Fundamental principle: Enzymes increase reaction rates by lowering the activation energy required to reach the transition state. They do not change the overall free-energy difference between reactants and products or alter the equilibrium constant of a reaction.

Examples of enzymes

  • Amylase: Catalyses the breakdown of starch.
  • Pepsin: Helps digest proteins in the stomach.
  • Lipase: Catalyses the hydrolysis of fats.
  • Catalase: Breaks down hydrogen peroxide.
  • DNA polymerase: Participates in DNA synthesis.
  • Hexokinase: Catalyses the phosphorylation of glucose.

03 · PROPERTIES OF ENZYMES

General properties of enzymes

1. High catalytic efficiency

Enzymes can accelerate reactions by very large factors. Some enzymes convert thousands or millions of substrate molecules per second under suitable conditions.

2. Specificity

Enzymes generally recognise particular substrates and catalyse particular types of chemical transformations. This specificity arises from the structure and chemical properties of the active site.

3. They are not permanently consumed

An enzyme participates in a reaction and is regenerated at the end of the catalytic cycle. It can therefore catalyse repeated reaction cycles, provided suitable conditions are maintained.

4. They have optimum conditions

Enzyme activity depends on environmental conditions such as temperature, pH, substrate concentration and ionic strength. Many enzymes have a characteristic optimum pH and temperature under defined experimental conditions.

5. They are affected by inhibitors and activators

Certain substances reduce enzyme activity, while others increase it. These interactions are important in metabolic regulation, pharmacology and laboratory analysis.

6. They do not change reaction equilibrium

Enzymes accelerate the approach to equilibrium but do not change the equilibrium position. They speed up both forward and reverse reactions by lowering the relevant activation barriers.

04 · ENZYME STRUCTURE

Structure and organisation of enzymes

Most enzymes are globular proteins with specific three-dimensional structures. Their catalytic activity depends on the correct organisation of amino acid residues and, in some cases, additional non-protein components.

The active site

The active site is the specialised region of an enzyme where substrate binding and catalysis occur. It contains amino acid residues positioned to recognise the substrate and facilitate the chemical reaction.

Simplified representation of an enzyme and its substrate-binding region. This is a conceptual illustration, not an atomic structure.

Substrate

A substrate is the reactant molecule upon which an enzyme acts. The substrate interacts with the enzyme's active site and is converted into one or more products.

Enzyme-substrate complex

During catalysis, an enzyme temporarily associates with its substrate to form an enzyme-substrate complex. This interaction positions chemical groups in a way that favours the reaction.

E + S ⇌ ES → E + P

E represents the enzyme, S the substrate, ES the enzyme-substrate complex and P the product.

Apoenzymes and holoenzymes

An apoenzyme is the protein component of an enzyme without its required cofactor. A holoenzyme is the complete, catalytically functional enzyme consisting of the apoenzyme together with its required cofactor or cofactors.

Cofactors

Cofactors are non-protein components required by certain enzymes for catalytic activity. They may be inorganic ions or organic molecules.

Coenzymes

Coenzymes are organic cofactors that participate in enzyme reactions, often by transferring electrons or specific chemical groups. Many are derived from vitamins.

Prosthetic groups

A prosthetic group is a non-protein component that is tightly or permanently associated with a protein. Examples include haem groups in certain enzymes and flavin groups in flavoproteins.

Metal ions in enzyme activity

Some enzymes require metal ions for proper structure or catalysis. Examples include zinc in carbonic anhydrase, magnesium in many kinases and iron in several redox enzymes.

05 · CLASSIFICATION

Classification and nomenclature of enzymes

Enzymes are classified according to the chemical reactions they catalyse. The International Union of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology (IUBMB) maintains the Enzyme Commission classification system.

Enzyme Commission numbers provide a systematic way of identifying enzyme-catalysed reactions. The modern classification recognises seven major classes.

ClassReaction catalysedExample
1. OxidoreductasesOxidation-reduction reactionsLactate dehydrogenase
2. TransferasesTransfer of functional groupsHexokinase, aminotransferases
3. HydrolasesBond cleavage using waterLipase, proteases, phosphatases
4. LyasesAddition or removal of groups without hydrolysis or oxidationFumarase, decarboxylases
5. IsomerasesIntramolecular rearrangementsPhosphoglucose isomerase
6. LigasesJoining of molecules, commonly coupled to energy useDNA ligase
7. TranslocasesMovement of ions or molecules across membranes or their separation within membranesATP synthase-associated translocation systems

1. Oxidoreductases

Oxidoreductases catalyse oxidation-reduction reactions involving electron transfer or changes in oxidation state. Dehydrogenases and oxidases belong to this group.

2. Transferases

Transferases move functional groups from one molecule to another. Kinases transfer phosphate groups, while aminotransferases transfer amino groups.

3. Hydrolases

Hydrolases catalyse the cleavage of chemical bonds through the addition of water. Digestive enzymes such as proteases, lipases and amylases are examples.

4. Lyases

Lyases catalyse the removal or addition of groups without hydrolysis or oxidation. They may form or break double bonds.

5. Isomerases

Isomerases catalyse rearrangements within a molecule, converting it into an isomer.

6. Ligases

Ligases join two molecules through the formation of a new chemical bond. Many ligase reactions are coupled to ATP or another energy source.

7. Translocases

Translocases catalyse the movement of ions or molecules across membranes or their separation within membranes. They are important in membrane transport and energy conversion.

Academic note: Older textbooks commonly describe six major enzyme classes. The seventh class, translocases, was added to the modern EC classification system. Students should recognise both systems when consulting different editions of textbooks.

06 · MECHANISM OF ACTION

How enzymes catalyse biochemical reactions

Enzymes accelerate reactions by providing a catalytic environment that makes the transition state easier to reach. They achieve this through several mechanisms that may operate together in a single enzyme.

Activation energy

Activation energy is the energy barrier that reactants must overcome to reach the transition state. Even when a reaction is energetically favourable, it may proceed slowly if this barrier is high.

The enzyme-catalysed pathway has a lower activation-energy barrier. The overall free-energy change remains unchanged.

Transition-state stabilisation

Enzymes bind and stabilise the transition state more effectively than the ground-state substrate. This lowers the activation energy required for the reaction.

Lock-and-key model

The lock-and-key model proposes that the active site has a relatively complementary shape to the substrate. The substrate fits into the active site in a manner similar to a key fitting into a lock.

Induced-fit model

The induced-fit model explains that substrate binding can cause changes in the shape and arrangement of the enzyme. These changes help position catalytic groups and improve the interaction with the transition state.

Important distinction: The induced-fit model is a more flexible description of enzyme-substrate recognition. Enzymes are not necessarily rigid structures; their conformations can change during substrate binding and catalysis.

Major catalytic strategies

1. Acid-base catalysis: Amino acid residues or other groups donate or accept protons during the reaction.

2. Covalent catalysis: The enzyme forms a temporary covalent bond with the substrate, creating an alternative reaction pathway.

3. Metal-ion catalysis: Metal ions help stabilise charges, orient substrates or participate in electron transfer.

4. Proximity and orientation effects: The enzyme brings reactants together and positions them correctly for reaction.

5. Electrostatic catalysis: Charged groups in the active site stabilise developing charges during the reaction.

07 · FACTORS AFFECTING ACTIVITY

Factors affecting enzyme activity

1. Temperature

Increasing temperature generally increases molecular movement and the frequency of effective molecular collisions. Enzyme activity therefore often increases as temperature rises towards an optimum.

Beyond the suitable temperature range, the enzyme may lose its functional structure through thermal denaturation. Its catalytic activity can decrease rapidly.

2. pH

pH affects the ionisation of amino acid residues in the active site and may alter the overall structure of the enzyme. Each enzyme has a characteristic pH-activity profile.

For example, pepsin functions effectively in the acidic environment of the stomach, whereas many pancreatic enzymes function better in a more alkaline intestinal environment.

3. Substrate concentration

At low substrate concentration, increasing substrate concentration usually increases reaction velocity. As more active sites become occupied, the rate approaches a maximum value known as Vmax.

4. Enzyme concentration

When substrate is present in sufficient excess and other conditions are constant, increasing enzyme concentration increases the reaction rate.

5. Inhibitors

Inhibitors reduce enzyme activity by interacting with the enzyme, its active site or another regulatory region.

6. Activators and cofactors

Certain molecules and ions are necessary for enzyme activity. Their availability may influence the rate of a reaction.

7. Ionic strength and solvent conditions

Salt concentration and the chemical environment can affect electrostatic interactions, protein folding and substrate binding. Extreme conditions may disrupt enzyme structure.

08 · ENZYME KINETICS

Introduction to enzyme kinetics

Enzyme kinetics is the quantitative study of the rates of enzyme-catalysed reactions and the factors that influence those rates.

Reaction velocity

Reaction velocity describes the rate at which substrate is converted into product. It may be measured as the change in substrate concentration or product concentration per unit time.

Reaction rate = Change in concentration ÷ Change in time

Michaelis-Menten equation

The Michaelis-Menten equation describes the relationship between initial reaction velocity and substrate concentration for many enzymes under appropriate conditions.

v = (Vmax × [S]) ÷ (Km + [S])
  • v: Initial reaction velocity.
  • Vmax: Maximum reaction velocity under the defined conditions.
  • [S]: Substrate concentration.
  • Km: Michaelis constant.

Maximum velocity (Vmax)

Vmax is the limiting reaction velocity approached when the enzyme is saturated with substrate. Under the appropriate model, nearly all enzyme active sites are occupied at substrate concentrations much greater than Km.

Michaelis constant (Km)

Km is the substrate concentration at which the initial reaction velocity is half of Vmax in the Michaelis-Menten model.

When [S] = Km, v = Vmax ÷ 2

Km is often used when comparing enzyme-substrate systems. However, it should not automatically be interpreted as a direct measure of binding affinity in every enzyme system. Its relationship to substrate binding depends on the kinetic mechanism.

Understanding the saturation curve

A typical Michaelis-Menten saturation curve showing the relationship between substrate concentration and initial reaction velocity.

Worked example

An enzyme has a Vmax of 100 µmol/min and a Km of 5 mmol/L. Calculate the initial velocity when the substrate concentration is 5 mmol/L.

v = (100 × 5) ÷ (5 + 5)
v = 500 ÷ 10 = 50 µmol/min

Therefore, the reaction velocity is 50 µmol/min, which is half of Vmax because the substrate concentration equals Km.

Turnover number (kcat)

The turnover number is the number of substrate molecules converted into product per enzyme molecule or active site per unit time when the enzyme is saturated with substrate.

kcat = Vmax ÷ [E]total

The catalytic efficiency of an enzyme-substrate pair is commonly expressed as kcat/Km.

Lineweaver-Burk plot

The Lineweaver-Burk plot is a double-reciprocal representation of Michaelis-Menten kinetics. It plots 1/v against 1/[S].

1/v = (Km/Vmax)(1/[S]) + 1/Vmax

The y-intercept is 1/Vmax, the x-intercept is -1/Km, and the slope is Km/Vmax.

Modern analytical note: Nonlinear regression of the original Michaelis-Menten equation is generally preferred for estimating kinetic parameters because double-reciprocal transformations can distort experimental error.

09 · ENZYME INHIBITION

Enzyme inhibitors and their mechanisms

Enzyme inhibition occurs when a substance decreases the catalytic activity of an enzyme. Inhibitors may act reversibly or irreversibly.

Reversible inhibition

Reversible inhibitors associate with enzymes through interactions that can be reversed. Common forms include competitive, uncompetitive and mixed inhibition.

1. Competitive inhibition

A competitive inhibitor competes with the substrate for binding to the enzyme's active site.

In the classical Michaelis-Menten model, competitive inhibition increases the apparent Km while Vmax remains unchanged. Increasing substrate concentration can overcome the inhibition.

2. Uncompetitive inhibition

An uncompetitive inhibitor binds only to the enzyme-substrate complex in the classical model. It decreases both apparent Km and Vmax.

3. Mixed inhibition

A mixed inhibitor can bind to both free enzyme and enzyme-substrate complex, but with different affinities. It decreases Vmax and changes apparent Km.

4. Non-competitive inhibition

Pure non-competitive inhibition is a special case of mixed inhibition in which the inhibitor binds equally well to the free enzyme and enzyme-substrate complex. Vmax decreases while Km remains unchanged.

Irreversible inhibition

Irreversible inhibitors inactivate enzymes through very stable interactions, commonly covalent modification or exceptionally tight binding. Their effects are not readily reversed by removing free inhibitor.

Some toxic chemicals and certain medicines act through irreversible enzyme inhibition.

Feedback inhibition

Feedback inhibition is a regulatory mechanism in which the final product of a metabolic pathway reduces the activity of an enzyme acting earlier in that pathway. It helps prevent unnecessary accumulation of products.

Clinical and pharmacological relevance: Enzyme inhibitors are important in drug development. Many medicines produce therapeutic effects by reducing the activity of selected enzymes. Their selectivity, dose and potential adverse effects must be evaluated.

10 · REGULATION

Regulation of enzyme activity

Cells regulate enzyme activity to coordinate metabolism with their energy needs, nutritional status and physiological conditions.

Allosteric regulation

Allosteric enzymes contain regulatory sites distinct from their active sites. Binding of a regulatory molecule can change enzyme conformation and alter catalytic activity.

Covalent modification

Enzymes may be regulated by reversible chemical modifications. Phosphorylation and dephosphorylation are important examples. Protein kinases add phosphate groups, while phosphatases remove them.

Proteolytic activation

Some enzymes are produced as inactive precursors called zymogens or proenzymes. They become active following specific proteolytic cleavage.

Examples include pepsinogen, trypsinogen and several blood-clotting proteins.

Enzyme synthesis and degradation

Cells can regulate enzyme abundance by changing gene expression, protein synthesis and protein degradation. This changes the amount of enzyme available to catalyse reactions.

Compartmentalisation

Different enzymes may be located in particular cellular compartments. This allows cells to separate metabolic pathways and regulate access to substrates and cofactors.

Importance of digestive enzymes

Digestive enzymes make dietary nutrients available for absorption. Without sufficient enzyme activity, digestion may be incomplete and nutritional problems may occur.

Clinical examples

  • Lactase deficiency: Reduced lactase activity can cause lactose intolerance.
  • Pancreatic insufficiency: Reduced digestive enzyme secretion can impair digestion and nutrient absorption.
  • Pancreatic inflammation: Serum lipase and sometimes amylase measurements may assist clinical assessment.

12 · CLINICAL ENZYMOLOGY

Clinical importance of enzymes

Clinical enzymology involves the measurement and interpretation of enzyme activities in biological samples, particularly blood serum or plasma. Enzyme results can provide useful information about tissue injury, organ function and disease.

Alanine aminotransferase (ALT)

ALT catalyses the transfer of an amino group between alanine and alpha-ketoglutarate, producing pyruvate and glutamate.

Increased serum ALT activity is commonly associated with liver cell injury. It must be interpreted with other clinical findings and laboratory results.

Aspartate aminotransferase (AST)

AST catalyses the reversible transfer of an amino group between aspartate and alpha-ketoglutarate. It is found in the liver, heart, skeletal muscle and other tissues.

Increased AST activity may occur in several forms of tissue injury. It is not specific to the liver.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP)

ALP catalyses the removal of phosphate groups from various molecules under alkaline conditions. It is present in tissues including the liver, bone and intestine.

Increased ALP may be associated with cholestatic liver conditions or increased bone activity. Additional tests help determine its likely source.

Gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT)

GGT participates in the metabolism and transfer of gamma-glutamyl groups. It is used alongside other tests in the assessment of hepatobiliary conditions.

Creatine kinase (CK)

CK catalyses the reversible transfer of a phosphate group between ATP and creatine. Increased serum CK activity may occur with skeletal muscle injury and certain other conditions.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)

LDH catalyses the reversible conversion of lactate to pyruvate with the interconversion of NAD⁺ and NADH. It is widely distributed in tissues, so increased serum activity is not specific to one organ.

Amylase and lipase

Amylase and lipase are digestive enzymes produced by the pancreas and other tissues. Serum lipase is commonly used in the assessment of suspected acute pancreatitis, with clinical findings.

13 · LABORATORY METHODS

Laboratory analysis of enzyme activity

Enzyme assays are analytical procedures used to measure enzyme activity under defined experimental conditions. They are important in clinical biochemistry, research, pharmaceutical development, food science and quality control.

Principle of an enzyme assay

An enzyme assay commonly measures the rate at which substrate disappears or product appears. The rate is used to estimate enzyme activity.

1. Spectrophotometric methods

Spectrophotometry measures the absorption of light by a sample at a selected wavelength. Some enzyme reactions produce or consume compounds that absorb ultraviolet or visible light.

NADH and NADPH absorb ultraviolet light strongly around 340 nm, while their oxidised forms absorb much less at this wavelength. This makes NADH-linked reactions useful for spectrophotometric enzyme assays.

A = ε × l × c

This is the Beer-Lambert relationship, where A is absorbance, ε is molar absorptivity, l is optical path length and c is concentration.

2. Colorimetric methods

Colorimetric assays measure colour intensity produced by an enzyme reaction. The colour may be measured using a spectrophotometer or colorimeter.

3. Fluorometric methods

Fluorometric assays measure fluorescence produced or altered during a reaction. They can provide high sensitivity when suitable fluorescent substrates or products are available.

4. Coupled enzyme assays

Some enzyme reactions do not directly produce a measurable signal. A second enzyme can be used to convert the first reaction's product into a substance that can be measured.

5. Electrochemical methods

Electrochemical methods measure changes in electrical properties such as current or potential associated with enzyme reactions. They are used in biosensors and analytical devices.

6. Immunoassays

Immunoassays use specific antigen-antibody interactions to detect or quantify selected proteins and enzymes. Depending on the method, the signal may be colour, fluorescence or luminescence.

7. Enzyme activity units

Enzyme activity is commonly expressed in units. One international unit (U) is the amount of enzyme that catalyses the conversion of one micromole of substrate per minute under specified conditions.

1 U = 1 µmol of substrate converted per minute

The katal (kat) is the SI unit of catalytic activity. One katal represents the conversion of one mole of substrate per second.

Factors affecting assay accuracy

  • Correct sample collection and handling.
  • Appropriate temperature and pH.
  • Suitable substrate concentration.
  • Calibrated analytical equipment.
  • Validated reagents and analytical procedures.
  • Appropriate blanks, controls and calibrators.
  • Correct timing and measurement of reaction rates.
  • Prevention of sample contamination and interference.

Quality assurance in enzyme analysis

Quality assurance involves planned activities that help ensure reliable laboratory results. Internal quality control, calibration, equipment maintenance, reagent verification and external quality assessment are important components.

Laboratory safety: Enzyme assays may involve biological samples, chemical reagents and potentially hazardous materials. Follow approved laboratory procedures, wear appropriate personal protective equipment and consult safety data sheets.

14 · INDUSTRIAL AND RESEARCH APPLICATIONS

Applications of enzymes in science and industry

Food processing

Enzymes are used in food production to improve texture, flavour, processing efficiency and product characteristics. Amylases are used in starch processing, while proteases may be used in selected food applications.

Pharmaceutical industry

Enzymes are used in drug manufacturing, diagnostic testing and therapeutic applications. Some enzymes are themselves used as medicines under controlled clinical conditions.

Biotechnology

Restriction enzymes, DNA polymerases and ligases are essential tools in molecular biology and genetic engineering. They support DNA analysis, amplification, cloning and other research procedures.

Detergent industry

Proteases, lipases and amylases are incorporated into some detergents to help break down protein, fat and starch stains.

Environmental science

Enzymes can support the breakdown or transformation of selected environmental pollutants. Enzyme-based approaches are investigated in bioremediation and environmental monitoring.

Agriculture

Enzymes are relevant to animal feed processing, plant physiology, crop research and the development of biological products for agricultural applications.

Artificial intelligence and enzyme research

Computational tools and artificial intelligence are increasingly used to predict protein structures, investigate enzyme-substrate interactions and support enzyme engineering. Experimental validation remains necessary before computational predictions are treated as established findings.

15 · CHAPTER SUMMARY

Key points to remember

  • Enzymology is the scientific study of enzymes.
  • Enzymes are biological catalysts that increase reaction rates.
  • Most enzymes are proteins, although catalytic RNA molecules exist.
  • Enzymes lower activation energy without changing reaction equilibrium.
  • The active site is the region responsible for substrate binding and catalysis.
  • Cofactors may be inorganic ions or organic molecules.
  • Coenzymes are organic cofactors, many of which are vitamin-derived.
  • The seven major EC classes are oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases, ligases and translocases.
  • The Michaelis-Menten equation describes the relationship between substrate concentration and initial reaction velocity for suitable enzyme systems.
  • Km is the substrate concentration at half Vmax in the Michaelis-Menten model.
  • Enzyme inhibitors may be competitive, uncompetitive, mixed or irreversible.
  • Enzyme activity is regulated through allosteric interactions, covalent modification and other mechanisms.
  • Clinical enzyme measurements can assist the investigation of tissue injury and disease.
  • Enzyme assays require proper calibration, controls and quality assurance.
