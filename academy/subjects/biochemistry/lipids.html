Topic 04: Lipids

Explore the chemistry of fats and oils, fatty acids, membrane lipids, cholesterol, lipid digestion, energy production, lipid synthesis and the clinical importance of lipid metabolism.

COMPREHENSIVE STUDY NOTES

1. Introduction to Lipids

Lipids are a diverse group of naturally occurring organic compounds that are generally insoluble or poorly soluble in water but soluble in non-polar organic solvents such as ether, chloroform and benzene. They are essential components of living organisms and perform structural, metabolic, protective and regulatory functions.

Unlike proteins and nucleic acids, lipids do not possess one common repeating monomer. Instead, they are grouped according to their physical and chemical properties. Some contain fatty acids, while others, such as steroids, have a different structural organisation.

Lipids occur in plant and animal tissues. Examples include vegetable oils, butter, animal fats, waxes, phospholipids in cell membranes, cholesterol in animal tissues and triglycerides stored in adipose tissue.

Central concept: Lipids are important because they store concentrated metabolic energy, form biological membranes, insulate the body, protect organs and participate in cell signalling.

1.1 General properties of lipids

  • Most lipids are hydrophobic or amphipathic.
  • Many are soluble in non-polar organic solvents.
  • They provide approximately 9 kcal of energy per gram when oxidised.
  • Some contain ester bonds that can undergo hydrolysis.
  • Some lipids contain phosphate groups, nitrogen or carbohydrate components.
  • They contribute to the fluidity and organisation of cell membranes.

1.2 Biological importance

Lipids provide long-term energy storage, supply essential fatty acids, assist the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K, and serve as precursors of steroid hormones, bile acids and signalling molecules. They also help maintain body temperature and protect internal organs.

2. Classification of Lipids

Lipids can be classified according to their chemical composition, structure and behaviour during hydrolysis. A useful traditional classification divides them into simple, compound and derived lipids.

ClassDescriptionExamples
Simple lipidsEsters of fatty acids with alcohols.Triacylglycerols and waxes.
Compound lipidsContain additional groups such as phosphate, carbohydrate or protein.Phospholipids and glycolipids.
Derived lipidsSubstances obtained from the hydrolysis or transformation of other lipids.Fatty acids, glycerol, steroids and fat-soluble vitamins.

2.1 Simple lipids

Simple lipids are esters formed from fatty acids and alcohols. Triacylglycerols are the most important energy-storage lipids. Waxes are formed when long-chain fatty acids combine with long-chain monohydric alcohols. Waxes provide waterproofing and protective coverings in plants and animals.

2.2 Compound or complex lipids

Compound lipids contain fatty acids and alcohol together with additional chemical groups. Phospholipids contain phosphate, while glycolipids contain carbohydrate groups. These molecules are especially important in biological membranes and cell recognition.

2.3 Derived lipids

Derived lipids include fatty acids, glycerol, sterols, steroid derivatives and other substances produced through lipid breakdown or modification. Cholesterol is an important sterol in animal cells.

2.4 Saponifiable and non-saponifiable lipids

Saponifiable lipids contain hydrolysable ester bonds and can react with strong alkali to produce soap. Triacylglycerols, waxes and many phospholipids fall into this category. Non-saponifiable lipids do not contain the relevant ester linkages in their basic structures. Steroids and many terpenes are examples.

3. Fatty Acids

Fatty acids are carboxylic acids containing hydrocarbon chains. Their general structure consists of a terminal carboxyl group (–COOH) attached to a hydrocarbon chain. They are important components of triacylglycerols, phospholipids and other lipids.

General representation: CH₃–(CH₂)n–COOH

Fatty acids vary in chain length, degree of unsaturation and configuration. These characteristics influence their melting points, physical states and biological properties.

3.1 Saturated fatty acids

Saturated fatty acids contain no carbon-carbon double bonds in their hydrocarbon chains. Their chains can pack relatively closely together, often giving them higher melting points.

Examples include palmitic acid (16:0) and stearic acid (18:0). They occur in animal fats and in several plant-derived fats.

3.2 Unsaturated fatty acids

Unsaturated fatty acids contain one or more carbon-carbon double bonds. Monounsaturated fatty acids contain one double bond, whereas polyunsaturated fatty acids contain two or more.

  • Oleic acid: 18:1, an example of a monounsaturated fatty acid.
  • Linoleic acid: 18:2, an omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acid.
  • Alpha-linolenic acid: 18:3, an omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid.
  • Arachidonic acid: 20:4, an omega-6 fatty acid.

Naturally occurring unsaturated fatty acids commonly have cis double bonds. These introduce bends into the hydrocarbon chains, reducing close packing and generally lowering melting points.

3.3 Essential fatty acids

Essential fatty acids cannot be synthesised in sufficient quantities by humans and must be obtained from the diet. Linoleic acid and alpha-linolenic acid are essential fatty acids.

Linoleic acid is a precursor of longer-chain omega-6 fatty acids, while alpha-linolenic acid can be converted, with limited efficiency, into longer-chain omega-3 fatty acids such as EPA and DHA. These fatty acids contribute to membrane function and the production of lipid mediators.

3.4 Omega nomenclature

Omega nomenclature identifies the position of the first double bond when counting from the methyl (omega) end of a fatty acid. For example, omega-3 fatty acids have their first double bond at the third carbon from that end.

3.5 Physical and chemical properties

  • Solubility in water decreases as the hydrocarbon chain becomes longer.
  • Unsaturation generally lowers the melting point.
  • Fatty acids can undergo esterification with alcohols.
  • Unsaturated fatty acids can undergo oxidation, producing rancidity.
  • Double bonds can react with halogens such as iodine or bromine.

4. Triacylglycerols: Fats and Oils

Triacylglycerols, also called triglycerides, are the major storage form of fat in humans and many other organisms. They consist of one glycerol molecule esterified with three fatty acid molecules.

A glycerol backbone joined to three fatty acid chains by ester bonds.

4.1 Formation of triglycerides

A triglyceride is formed by three esterification reactions between glycerol and fatty acids. Each ester bond forms with the release of one water molecule.

Glycerol + 3 fatty acids → Triacylglycerol + 3H₂O

4.2 Functions of triglycerides

  • Long-term energy storage.
  • Thermal insulation beneath the skin.
  • Mechanical protection and cushioning of organs.
  • Provision of fatty acids during energy demand.
  • Support of normal metabolic functions.

4.3 Difference between fats and oils

Fats are generally solid or semi-solid at room temperature, while oils are generally liquid. This difference is strongly influenced by fatty acid composition. Oils often contain a greater proportion of unsaturated fatty acids, whereas many solid fats contain more saturated fatty acids.

5. Phospholipids

Phospholipids are complex lipids containing phosphate groups. Many are amphipathic, meaning that they possess both a hydrophilic (water-attracting) region and hydrophobic (water-repelling) region. This property allows them to form the lipid bilayer of biological membranes.

5.1 Main types of phospholipids

  • Glycerophospholipids: contain glycerol, fatty acids, phosphate and a polar head group.
  • Sphingomyelin: contains a sphingosine backbone, a fatty acid and a phosphocholine or phosphoethanolamine head group.

5.2 Important glycerophospholipids

  • Phosphatidylcholine (lecithin).
  • Phosphatidylethanolamine.
  • Phosphatidylserine.
  • Phosphatidylinositol.
  • Cardiolipin, particularly important in the inner mitochondrial membrane.

5.3 Membrane bilayer formation

Simplified phospholipid bilayer: polar heads face aqueous environments while fatty acid tails point inward.

5.4 Biological functions

  • Form the structural basis of cell membranes.
  • Regulate membrane fluidity and permeability.
  • Participate in intracellular signalling.
  • Support the activity of membrane proteins.
  • Contribute to lipoprotein and lung surfactant structure.

6. Glycolipids and Sphingolipids

Glycolipids are lipids containing carbohydrate groups. They are important components of cell membranes, especially on the outer surface of the plasma membrane. They participate in cell recognition, communication and immune interactions.

Sphingolipids are built around a sphingosine backbone. They include ceramide, sphingomyelin and glycosphingolipids. They are particularly abundant in nervous tissue and contribute to the organisation of cell membranes and myelin.

Clinical relevance

Defects in lysosomal enzymes responsible for sphingolipid breakdown can cause sphingolipidoses. Examples include Gaucher disease, Tay-Sachs disease and Niemann-Pick disease. These conditions involve abnormal accumulation of specific lipid substrates in tissues.

7. Cholesterol and Steroids

Cholesterol is a sterol containing four fused hydrocarbon rings, a hydroxyl group and a hydrocarbon side chain. It is an essential component of animal cell membranes and a precursor of several biologically important compounds.

7.1 Functions of cholesterol

  • Modifies the fluidity and permeability of animal cell membranes.
  • Serves as a precursor of steroid hormones.
  • Serves as a precursor of bile acids and bile salts.
  • Serves as a precursor of vitamin D synthesis in the skin.

7.2 Steroid hormones

Steroid hormones are synthesised from cholesterol. Important examples include cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone, oestrogens and progesterone. They regulate metabolism, stress responses, electrolyte balance, reproductive processes and development.

7.3 Bile acids and bile salts

The liver converts cholesterol into primary bile acids. These are conjugated with glycine or taurine to form bile salts, which assist the emulsification and digestion of dietary fats in the small intestine. Most bile acids are subsequently reabsorbed through enterohepatic circulation.

8. Digestion and Absorption of Lipids

Dietary lipids must be processed before most can be absorbed. Digestion occurs mainly in the small intestine, although some initial lipid digestion occurs in the mouth and stomach.

8.1 Mouth and stomach

Lingual and gastric lipases contribute to the early digestion of dietary triglycerides. Their activity is particularly relevant in infants and in certain digestive conditions.

8.2 Small intestine and bile salts

Bile salts are released into the small intestine. Their detergent-like action helps emulsify large fat droplets into smaller droplets, increasing the surface area available to pancreatic enzymes.

8.3 Pancreatic enzymes

  • Pancreatic lipase: hydrolyses triglycerides mainly at the sn-1 and sn-3 positions, producing 2-monoacylglycerol and free fatty acids.
  • Phospholipase A₂: hydrolyses a fatty acid from the sn-2 position of many phospholipids.
  • Cholesterol esterase: hydrolyses cholesterol esters and other lipid esters.

8.4 Micelle formation and absorption

The products of lipid digestion combine with bile salts to form mixed micelles. These transport lipid digestion products to the intestinal brush border, where they can enter intestinal cells.

Inside enterocytes, long-chain fatty acids and monoacylglycerols are reassembled into triacylglycerols. These are packaged with cholesterol, phospholipids and apolipoproteins into chylomicrons.

8.5 Transport through lymph and blood

Chylomicrons enter intestinal lymphatic vessels called lacteals and subsequently reach the bloodstream. In contrast, short- and medium-chain fatty acids can be absorbed more directly into portal blood, often travelling bound to albumin.

Clinical connection: Defective bile production, bile obstruction or pancreatic enzyme deficiency can impair fat digestion and absorption, potentially producing steatorrhoea and deficiencies of fat-soluble vitamins.

9. Lipid Transport and Lipoproteins

Because most lipids are poorly soluble in blood, they are transported in association with proteins in particles called lipoproteins. These particles contain a hydrophobic core of triglycerides and cholesteryl esters surrounded by phospholipids, free cholesterol and apolipoproteins.

LipoproteinMain role
ChylomicronsTransport dietary triglycerides from the intestine to tissues.
VLDLTransport triglycerides synthesised by the liver to peripheral tissues.
IDLIntermediate particles formed during VLDL metabolism.
LDLDelivers cholesterol to peripheral tissues through LDL receptors.
HDLParticipates in reverse cholesterol transport and lipid exchange.

9.1 Important apolipoproteins

  • ApoB-48: structural protein of chylomicrons.
  • ApoB-100: structural protein of VLDL, IDL and LDL; also binds the LDL receptor.
  • ApoC-II: activates lipoprotein lipase.
  • ApoE: helps hepatic uptake of remnant particles.
  • ApoA-I: major structural protein of HDL and activator of LCAT.

9.2 Lipoprotein lipase

Lipoprotein lipase is an enzyme located on the luminal surface of capillary endothelial cells, especially in adipose tissue and skeletal and cardiac muscle. It hydrolyses triglycerides in circulating chylomicrons and VLDL, releasing fatty acids for tissue uptake.

10. Lipid Metabolism

Lipid metabolism includes the synthesis, storage, breakdown and utilisation of fatty acids and other lipids. It is regulated according to nutritional state, hormonal signals and cellular energy requirements.

10.1 Lipolysis

Lipolysis is the breakdown of stored triacylglycerols in adipose tissue into fatty acids and glycerol. Adipose triglyceride lipase initiates the process, hormone-sensitive lipase contributes to subsequent hydrolysis, and monoacylglycerol lipase completes it.

Catecholamines stimulate adipose lipolysis through signalling pathways involving cyclic AMP and protein kinase A. Insulin generally suppresses lipolysis in the fed state.

10.2 Transport of fatty acids into mitochondria

Long-chain fatty acids are activated to fatty acyl-CoA in an ATP-dependent reaction. Their entry into the mitochondrial matrix requires the carnitine shuttle, involving carnitine palmitoyltransferase I, carnitine-acylcarnitine translocase and carnitine palmitoyltransferase II.

10.3 Beta-oxidation of fatty acids

Beta-oxidation is the major pathway by which many fatty acids are broken down to produce acetyl-CoA. It occurs mainly in the mitochondrial matrix. Each cycle shortens the fatty acyl-CoA chain by two carbon atoms and produces reducing equivalents.

Four recurring reactions of beta-oxidation

  1. Oxidation: acyl-CoA dehydrogenase forms a trans double bond and produces FADH₂.
  2. Hydration: enoyl-CoA hydratase adds water across the double bond.
  3. Oxidation: hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase forms a keto group and produces NADH.
  4. Thiolysis: thiolase cleaves the molecule, releasing acetyl-CoA and a fatty acyl-CoA shortened by two carbons.
Fatty acyl-CoA → FADH₂ + NADH + acetyl-CoA + shortened fatty acyl-CoA

10.4 Energy yield from palmitate

Palmitate is a 16-carbon saturated fatty acid. Its complete beta-oxidation produces eight acetyl-CoA, seven NADH and seven FADH₂.

Using modern approximate P/O ratios of 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 ATP per FADH₂, the eight acetyl-CoA yield approximately 80 ATP through the citric acid cycle, while the seven NADH and seven FADH₂ yield approximately 28 ATP. Subtracting the two ATP equivalents used in fatty acid activation gives a net yield of approximately 106 ATP.

Calculation: 80 + 17.5 + 10.5 − 2 = 106 ATP. This is the commonly taught estimate for complete oxidation of palmitate under these assumptions.

10.5 Ketone body formation

During prolonged fasting, carbohydrate restriction or uncontrolled diabetes, the liver may produce increased amounts of ketone bodies from acetyl-CoA. The principal ketone bodies are acetoacetate, beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetone.

Ketone bodies can be used as an energy source by extrahepatic tissues, including the brain during prolonged fasting. The liver produces ketone bodies but cannot use them for energy because it lacks the enzyme SCOT (succinyl-CoA:acetoacetate CoA transferase).

10.6 Fatty acid synthesis

Fatty acid synthesis occurs mainly in the cytosol of liver, adipose tissue and lactating mammary tissue. Acetyl-CoA is converted into malonyl-CoA by acetyl-CoA carboxylase, a biotin-dependent enzyme. Fatty acid synthase then uses acetyl-CoA, malonyl-CoA and NADPH to build fatty acid chains.

Acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + ATP → Malonyl-CoA + ADP + Pi

The main product of the fatty acid synthase system is palmitate (16:0). Further elongation and desaturation can occur through additional enzyme systems.

10.7 Regulation of fatty acid synthesis

  • Insulin promotes lipogenesis in the fed state.
  • Glucagon and adrenaline generally oppose fatty acid synthesis.
  • Acetyl-CoA carboxylase is a major regulatory enzyme.
  • Malonyl-CoA inhibits CPT-I, reducing mitochondrial entry of long-chain fatty acids during active synthesis.

10.8 Cholesterol synthesis

Cholesterol is synthesised mainly in the liver and other tissues through a pathway beginning with acetyl-CoA. HMG-CoA reductase converts HMG-CoA to mevalonate and is a major regulatory enzyme of cholesterol synthesis. Statin medicines lower cholesterol synthesis by inhibiting this enzyme.

11. Regulation of Lipid Metabolism

Lipid metabolism changes according to the body's nutritional and hormonal state. Insulin, glucagon and catecholamines are important regulators.

12. Laboratory Analysis of Lipids

Lipid analysis is important in clinical biochemistry, food science, pharmaceutical research, nutritional studies and quality assurance. Different analytical techniques are selected according to the lipid being measured and the purpose of the investigation.

12.1 Extraction of lipids

Lipids can be extracted from biological and food samples using suitable solvent systems. Traditional methods include the Folch chloroform-methanol extraction and the Bligh-Dyer method. Sample preparation must be appropriate for the material being analysed.

12.2 Gravimetric determination

In gravimetric analysis, lipids are extracted, the solvent is removed under controlled conditions, and the residue is weighed. The result can be expressed as the percentage of lipid in the original sample.

Lipid (%) = (Mass of extracted lipid ÷ Mass of sample) × 100

12.3 Acid value

Acid value is the number of milligrams of potassium hydroxide required to neutralise the free fatty acids present in one gram of fat or oil. It is used to assess hydrolytic deterioration.

12.4 Saponification value

Saponification value is the number of milligrams of potassium hydroxide required to saponify one gram of fat. It gives useful information about the average molecular mass of the fatty acids present in a fat or oil.

12.5 Iodine value

Iodine value measures the amount of iodine, expressed in grams, absorbed by 100 grams of fat or oil under specified test conditions. It is an indicator of the degree of unsaturation.

12.6 Peroxide value

Peroxide value estimates primary oxidation products, particularly peroxides and hydroperoxides, in fats and oils. It is used in monitoring oxidative rancidity.

12.7 Chromatographic methods

  • Thin-layer chromatography (TLC): separates lipid classes according to their interactions with the stationary and mobile phases.
  • Gas chromatography (GC): commonly analyses fatty acid methyl esters to determine fatty acid composition.
  • High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC): separates and measures selected lipid compounds.
  • Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS): supports detailed lipid identification and lipidomics.

12.8 Enzymatic measurement of serum lipids

Clinical laboratories commonly measure triglycerides and cholesterol using enzymatic colorimetric methods. Cholesterol assays may involve cholesterol esterase, cholesterol oxidase and a peroxidase-based colour reaction. Triglyceride assays commonly involve lipase, glycerol kinase and coupled enzyme reactions that generate a measurable colour signal.

12.9 Lipid profile

A lipid profile is a group of blood tests used to assess circulating lipid-related measurements. It commonly includes total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, triglycerides and calculated or directly measured LDL cholesterol. Non-HDL cholesterol may also be reported.

MeasurementWhat it representsClinical use
Total cholesterolCholesterol carried in different lipoproteins.Cardiovascular risk assessment.
HDL-CCholesterol associated with HDL particles.Part of cardiovascular risk assessment.

Laboratory caution: Interpretation of lipid results depends on the clinical context, assay method, patient preparation and current professional guidelines. A laboratory result should not be interpreted in isolation.

13. Clinical Significance of Lipids

13.1 Dyslipidaemia

Dyslipidaemia refers to abnormal concentrations or patterns of circulating lipids and lipoproteins. It may involve elevated LDL cholesterol, increased triglycerides, low HDL cholesterol or combinations of these changes.

13.2 Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis is a chronic disease of the arterial wall involving lipid accumulation, inflammation, cellular responses and plaque formation. Retention of apoB-containing lipoproteins in the arterial wall is a major initiating event. Plaque progression can narrow arteries or contribute to thrombosis, increasing the risk of myocardial infarction and stroke.

13.3 Fatty liver disease

Excess accumulation of triglycerides in liver cells can result in hepatic steatosis. Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease is associated with factors such as insulin resistance, obesity and metabolic abnormalities. Disease severity varies, and some individuals develop inflammation and fibrosis.

13.4 Ketosis and ketoacidosis

Ketosis occurs when ketone body production increases. Diabetic ketoacidosis is a serious metabolic emergency, usually associated with significant insulin deficiency and excess ketone production. It causes metabolic acidosis and requires urgent medical treatment.

13.5 Essential fatty acid deficiency

Deficiency of essential fatty acids can affect skin integrity, growth and normal physiological functions. It is uncommon in people consuming adequate diets but may occur in certain nutritional or medical circumstances.

13.6 Inherited lipid disorders

Genetic abnormalities affecting lipoprotein production, transport or clearance can cause inherited dyslipidaemias. Familial hypercholesterolaemia, for example, is commonly associated with impaired LDL receptor pathway function and markedly increased LDL cholesterol.

14. Lipids in Nutrition and Food Science

Dietary lipids provide energy and essential fatty acids and assist the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins. The nutritional effects of fats depend on their fatty acid composition, overall dietary pattern and the foods that replace them.

14.1 Food sources

  • Olive, soybean, sunflower and palm oils.
  • Groundnuts, walnuts and other nuts.
  • Avocado and seeds.
  • Fish and seafood.
  • Eggs, dairy products and meat.

14.2 Rancidity

Rancidity is the deterioration of fats and oils, producing undesirable odours, flavours and chemical changes. Hydrolytic rancidity results from the release of free fatty acids, while oxidative rancidity involves reactions between oxygen and unsaturated fatty acids.

14.3 Prevention of lipid oxidation

  • Limit exposure to oxygen where appropriate.
  • Protect products from excessive heat and light.
  • Use suitable airtight packaging.
  • Apply approved antioxidants where appropriate.
  • Maintain appropriate storage conditions.

14.4 Hydrogenation

Hydrogenation adds hydrogen to carbon-carbon double bonds in unsaturated oils. Partial hydrogenation can produce trans fatty acids, which are associated with adverse cardiovascular effects. Industrial trans fats have therefore been restricted or eliminated in many food regulatory systems.

15. Research and Modern Applications of Lipid Science

  • Lipidomics: large-scale study of lipid molecules and their changes in biological systems.
  • Drug delivery: lipid nanoparticles and liposomes can carry therapeutic substances.
  • Biotechnology: engineered lipid systems are used in research and pharmaceutical development.
  • Food technology: lipid analysis supports product quality, shelf-life assessment and nutritional evaluation.
  • Clinical research: lipid biomarkers help investigate metabolic and cardiovascular diseases.
  • Data science and AI: computational analysis can identify patterns in lipidomics and metabolic datasets.

16. Summary of Important Concepts

  • Lipids are diverse compounds that are generally poorly soluble in water.
  • Simple lipids include triacylglycerols and waxes.
  • Fatty acids may be saturated, monounsaturated or polyunsaturated.
  • Linoleic acid and alpha-linolenic acid are essential fatty acids.
  • Triacylglycerols are major energy-storage molecules.
  • Phospholipids form the foundation of biological membranes.
  • Cholesterol is a membrane component and precursor of steroid hormones and bile acids.
  • Dietary lipid digestion involves bile salts and pancreatic enzymes.
  • Lipoproteins transport lipids through the bloodstream.
  • Beta-oxidation produces acetyl-CoA, NADH and FADH₂.
  • Fatty acid synthesis and cholesterol synthesis are regulated metabolic pathways.
  • Lipid laboratory methods include titration, chromatography and enzymatic assays.
