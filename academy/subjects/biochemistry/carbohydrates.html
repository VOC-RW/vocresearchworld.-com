02 · INTRODUCTION

What are carbohydrates?

Carbohydrates are an important class of biological molecules composed primarily of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. They occur widely in plants, animals and microorganisms and perform essential structural, metabolic and energy-related functions.

In introductory biochemistry, carbohydrates are commonly described as polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or substances that yield these compounds on hydrolysis. A polyhydroxy compound contains several hydroxyl (-OH) groups, while an aldehyde or ketone represents a characteristic carbonyl group.

Glucose, fructose, sucrose, lactose, starch, glycogen and cellulose are examples of carbohydrates. Although these molecules belong to the same broad chemical family, they differ considerably in molecular size, structure, solubility and biological function.

Important concept: Carbohydrates are not simply substances that provide energy. They also contribute to cellular recognition, molecular communication, structural support and the organisation of biological membranes.

General chemical composition

Many simple carbohydrates have an approximate empirical composition represented by (CH₂O)n. This relationship helped establish the historical term carbohydrate, meaning hydrated carbon. However, this formula is not universal: deoxyribose, amino sugars and other modified carbohydrates do not necessarily follow it.

For example, glucose has the molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₆, while ribose has the formula C₅H₁₀O₅.

03 · CLASSIFICATION

Classification of carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are classified mainly according to the number of sugar units they contain and the products formed when larger carbohydrate molecules undergo hydrolysis.

A. Monosaccharides

Monosaccharides are the simplest carbohydrates. They consist of a single sugar unit and cannot be hydrolysed into smaller carbohydrate molecules.

Examples include glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose and deoxyribose. They are generally soluble in water and many possess a sweet taste.

Classification by carbonyl group

  • Aldoses: Monosaccharides containing an aldehyde group in their open-chain form. Glucose and ribose are examples.
  • Ketoses: Monosaccharides containing a ketone group in their open-chain form. Fructose is a common example.

04 · MOLECULAR STRUCTURE

Structure of monosaccharides

Monosaccharides can exist in open-chain and cyclic forms. In aqueous solution, many five- and six-carbon sugars occur predominantly as cyclic structures formed through an intramolecular reaction between their carbonyl and hydroxyl groups.

Open-chain and ring forms of glucose

Glucose is an aldohexose containing six carbon atoms. Its open-chain structure contains an aldehyde group at carbon 1 and hydroxyl groups attached to other carbon atoms. In solution, glucose commonly forms a six-membered ring called a pyranose ring.

Open-chain glucose can cyclise to form a cyclic hemiacetal.

Furanose and pyranose forms

  • Furanose: A cyclic sugar structure with a five-membered ring.
  • Pyranose: A cyclic sugar structure with a six-membered ring.

Ribose commonly occurs in furanose form in nucleotides, while glucose commonly occurs in pyranose form.

Anomers and the anomeric carbon

Cyclisation creates a new stereogenic centre at the carbon that was originally part of the carbonyl group. This carbon is called the anomeric carbon.

The two possible configurations are called alpha (α) and beta (β) anomers. In the common Haworth representation of D-glucopyranose, the anomeric hydroxyl group is drawn below the ring in the alpha form and above the ring in the beta form.

Mutarotation

Mutarotation is the change in optical rotation that occurs when alpha and beta anomers interconvert in solution through the open-chain form until an equilibrium mixture is reached.

Examination point: Anomers differ specifically in configuration at the anomeric carbon. Epimers differ in configuration at only one chiral carbon other than the anomeric distinction used to define anomers.

05 · STEREOCHEMISTRY

Isomerism in carbohydrates

Isomerism occurs when compounds have the same molecular formula but differ in the arrangement of their atoms. Carbohydrate stereochemistry is important because even small structural differences can produce major differences in biological recognition and enzyme activity.

Structural isomerism

Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atomic connectivity. Glucose and fructose both have the formula C₆H₁₂O₆, but glucose is an aldose while fructose is a ketose.

D and L configurations

The D and L designations describe configuration relative to glyceraldehyde. In a Fischer projection, the position of the hydroxyl group on the highest-numbered chiral carbon determines the designation.

D does not automatically mean dextrorotatory, and L does not automatically mean levorotatory. Optical rotation must be determined experimentally.

Epimers

Epimers are diastereomers that differ in configuration at only one specific chiral carbon.

  • Glucose and galactose are C-4 epimers.
  • Glucose and mannose are C-2 epimers.

Optical activity

Many monosaccharides possess chiral carbon atoms and can rotate plane-polarised light. The direction and magnitude of rotation are measured using a polarimeter.

06 · GLYCOSIDIC BONDS

Formation of glycosidic linkages

A glycosidic bond is a covalent linkage formed when the anomeric carbon of a sugar becomes bonded to another group, commonly the oxygen atom of another sugar's hydroxyl group.

In the formation of many common disaccharides, two monosaccharides join through a condensation reaction accompanied by the removal of water. Hydrolysis is the reverse process, in which water is used to break the glycosidic bond.

Monosaccharide + Monosaccharide ⇌ Disaccharide + H₂O

Examples of glycosidic linkages

  • Maltose: Two glucose units joined by an α(1→4) linkage.
  • Lactose: Galactose and glucose joined by a β(1→4) linkage.
  • Sucrose: Glucose and fructose joined through their anomeric carbons, commonly described as α(1→2)β.

The type of glycosidic linkage influences the shape, digestibility and biological function of a carbohydrate.

07 · CHEMICAL PROPERTIES

Physical and chemical properties

Solubility

Many monosaccharides and small oligosaccharides are soluble in water because their hydroxyl groups form hydrogen bonds with water molecules. Large polysaccharides may have limited solubility or form colloidal dispersions, depending on their molecular structure.

Sweetness

Many simple sugars taste sweet, although sweetness varies among different carbohydrates. Most structural polysaccharides are not noticeably sweet.

Reducing properties

A reducing sugar has a free anomeric carbon capable of accessing an open-chain form under suitable conditions. This allows it to reduce certain chemical reagents.

Glucose, fructose, lactose and maltose give positive results in common reducing-sugar tests. Fructose is a ketose, but it can behave as a reducing sugar in alkaline test conditions because it undergoes chemical rearrangement.

Sucrose is non-reducing because both anomeric carbons participate in its glycosidic linkage.

Hydrolysis

Hydrolysis breaks glycosidic bonds using water. For example, maltose can be hydrolysed to produce two glucose molecules, while sucrose produces glucose and fructose.

Oxidation and reduction

The aldehyde group of an aldose can be oxidised to form an acid under appropriate conditions. Carbonyl groups can also be reduced to form sugar alcohols, such as sorbitol from glucose.

Caramelisation and Maillard reaction

  • Caramelisation: Thermal decomposition and transformation of sugars during heating.
  • Maillard reaction: A complex set of reactions between reducing sugars and amino groups, contributing to browning and flavour development in foods.

08 · POLYSACCHARIDE CHEMISTRY

Starch, glycogen and cellulose

Starch

Starch is the principal storage polysaccharide in many plants. It consists of two major components:

  • Amylose: Mainly linear chains of glucose units linked by α(1→4) bonds.
  • Amylopectin: Branched glucose chains with α(1→4) bonds along the chains and α(1→6) bonds at branch points.

Starch is an important dietary carbohydrate and is found in foods such as rice, maize, yam, potatoes and cereals.

Glycogen

Glycogen is the major storage polysaccharide in animals. It is especially abundant in the liver and skeletal muscles. Its structure resembles amylopectin but has more frequent branching.

Liver glycogen helps maintain blood glucose between meals, while muscle glycogen provides a local source of glucose units for energy production during muscle activity.

Cellulose

Cellulose is a structural polysaccharide in plant cell walls. It consists of glucose units joined by β(1→4) glycosidic bonds. Its straight chains form strong fibres through extensive hydrogen bonding.

Humans do not produce cellulase, the enzyme required to digest cellulose. Therefore, cellulose contributes to dietary fibre rather than serving as a major source of digestible glucose in humans.

09 · BIOLOGICAL FUNCTIONS

Functions of carbohydrates in living organisms

1. Energy production

Glucose is an important metabolic fuel. Cells can break it down through glycolysis and subsequent pathways to produce ATP, the principal energy currency used by many cellular processes.

2. Energy storage

Plants store carbohydrate mainly as starch, while animals store it mainly as glycogen. These storage molecules allow organisms to retain glucose units for future use.

3. Structural support

Cellulose provides strength to plant cell walls. Chitin provides structural support in fungal cell walls and the exoskeletons of many arthropods.

4. Genetic material

Ribose is a component of RNA, while deoxyribose is a component of DNA. These pentose sugars form part of the nucleotide structures required for genetic information storage and expression.

5. Cell recognition and communication

Carbohydrate chains attached to proteins and lipids form glycoproteins and glycolipids. They contribute to cell recognition, adhesion and communication.

6. Precursors for biosynthesis

Carbohydrate metabolism supplies carbon skeletons required for the synthesis of amino acids, nucleotides, fatty acids and other cellular molecules.

10 · DIGESTION AND ABSORPTION

Digestion of carbohydrates in humans

Dietary carbohydrates include starch, sugars and non-digestible fibre. Digestible carbohydrates are broken down into monosaccharides before absorption through the intestinal epithelium.

Mouth

Digestion begins in the mouth through the action of salivary α-amylase. This enzyme hydrolyses internal α(1→4) glycosidic bonds in starch and glycogen, producing smaller carbohydrate fragments.

Stomach

The acidic environment of the stomach reduces the activity of salivary amylase. Therefore, carbohydrate digestion is limited in the stomach compared with the small intestine.

Small intestine

Pancreatic α-amylase continues starch digestion in the small intestine. It produces maltose, maltotriose and α-limit dextrins.

Enzymes located on the intestinal brush border complete the digestion:

  • Maltase and related glucosidases: Break down maltose and other small glucose-containing carbohydrates.
  • Sucrase: Breaks sucrose into glucose and fructose.
  • Lactase: Breaks lactose into glucose and galactose.
  • Isomaltase: Helps hydrolyse α(1→6) branch linkages in limit dextrins.

Absorption of monosaccharides

Glucose and galactose are taken into intestinal epithelial cells mainly through the sodium-dependent transporter SGLT1. Fructose enters mainly through GLUT5 by facilitated diffusion. These monosaccharides leave the cells across the basolateral membrane primarily through GLUT2 and enter the portal circulation.

Clinical connection: Reduced lactase activity can result in lactose malabsorption. Undigested lactose may be fermented by intestinal bacteria, producing gas and contributing to abdominal discomfort and diarrhoea in affected individuals.

11 · CARBOHYDRATE METABOLISM

Overview of carbohydrate metabolism

Carbohydrate metabolism includes the biochemical pathways through which carbohydrates are broken down, stored, mobilised and converted into other metabolites.

Glycolysis

Glycolysis is a series of enzyme-catalysed reactions occurring in the cytosol. It converts one molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, producing ATP and NADH.

Glucose → 2 Pyruvate + 2 ATP (net) + 2 NADH

Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate can be converted into acetyl-CoA and enter the citric acid cycle. Under certain anaerobic or oxygen-limited conditions, cells can convert pyruvate into lactate to regenerate NAD⁺ required for continued glycolysis.

Glycogenesis

Glycogenesis is the synthesis of glycogen from glucose units. It occurs mainly in the liver and skeletal muscle and is stimulated by insulin in appropriate metabolic conditions.

Glycogenolysis

Glycogenolysis is the breakdown of glycogen. It releases glucose-1-phosphate, which can be converted into glucose-6-phosphate. In the liver, glucose can ultimately be released into the blood through the action of glucose-6-phosphatase. Skeletal muscle uses its glycogen primarily for its own energy needs.

Gluconeogenesis

Gluconeogenesis is the synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors, including lactate, glycerol and certain amino acids. It occurs mainly in the liver and, during prolonged fasting, also contributes in the kidneys.

Citric acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation

When oxygen is available, acetyl-CoA derived from carbohydrate metabolism can be oxidised through the citric acid cycle. Reduced coenzymes transfer electrons to the respiratory chain, supporting ATP synthesis through oxidative phosphorylation.

Important distinction: Glycolysis breaks down glucose, glycogenesis builds glycogen, glycogenolysis breaks down glycogen, and gluconeogenesis produces glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors.

12 · REGULATION

Hormonal regulation of carbohydrate metabolism

Insulin

Insulin is secreted by pancreatic beta cells in response to appropriate metabolic signals, including increased blood glucose. It promotes glucose uptake in insulin- responsive tissues, especially skeletal muscle and adipose tissue, and supports glycogen synthesis.

Glucagon

Glucagon is secreted by pancreatic alpha cells and helps maintain blood glucose during fasting. It stimulates hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis.

Adrenaline

Adrenaline supports rapid energy mobilisation during stress and physical activity. It can stimulate glycogen breakdown in the liver and skeletal muscle.

13 · LABORATORY BIOCHEMISTRY

Laboratory analysis of carbohydrates

Carbohydrates can be investigated using qualitative chemical reactions and quantitative analytical techniques. The method selected depends on the substance being analysed, the required sensitivity and the purpose of the investigation.

Molisch's test

Molisch's test is a general qualitative test for carbohydrates. Concentrated sulphuric acid dehydrates carbohydrate compounds to form derivatives that react with α-naphthol, producing a violet or purple interface.

Benedict's test

Benedict's test detects reducing sugars. Under alkaline heating conditions, reducing sugars reduce blue copper(II) ions to copper(I) oxide, which may form a coloured precipitate.

Depending on the concentration, the observed colour may change from green through yellow and orange to brick-red. The test is qualitative or semi-quantitative rather than a precise glucose measurement.

Iodine test

Iodine solution is used to investigate starch. Amylose commonly produces a deep blue-black colour. The colour response depends on the structure of the polysaccharide and the conditions of the test.

Enzymatic glucose assay

Clinical laboratories commonly use enzymatic methods to determine glucose concentration. Examples include glucose oxidase-based methods and hexokinase-based methods. These methods provide greater analytical specificity than general reducing-sugar tests.

Other analytical methods

  • Colorimetry and spectrophotometry: Measure absorbance associated with a coloured reaction product.
  • Chromatography: Separates carbohydrate components in mixtures.
  • High-performance liquid chromatography: Can separate and quantify sugars using suitable detection systems.
  • Polarimetry: Measures optical rotation of optically active compounds.
  • Refractometry: Can estimate soluble solids in certain food and beverage samples.

Laboratory safety: Concentrated acids and heated chemical reagents require appropriate personal protective equipment, trained supervision and approved laboratory procedures. Never perform these tests without suitable safety controls.

14 · MEDICAL AND INDUSTRIAL APPLICATIONS

Clinical, nutritional and industrial importance

Clinical biochemistry

Blood glucose measurement is important in the assessment and monitoring of disorders of glucose regulation, including diabetes mellitus. Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) provides information about average glycaemic exposure over approximately the preceding two to three months, interpreted in the appropriate clinical context.

Nutrition

Carbohydrates provide an important source of dietary energy. Whole grains, fruits, vegetables and legumes can supply carbohydrates together with fibre, micronutrients and other beneficial compounds.

Food and beverage industries

Carbohydrates are used as sweeteners, thickeners, stabilisers, fermentation substrates and texture modifiers in food production.

Biotechnology and fermentation

Microorganisms use carbohydrates as substrates in fermentation processes. Depending on the organism and process conditions, products may include ethanol, organic acids, enzymes and other useful metabolites.

Pharmaceutical applications

Carbohydrate-based compounds are used in certain pharmaceutical formulations, biological recognition systems and drug delivery technologies.

15 · CHAPTER SUMMARY

Key points to remember

  • Carbohydrates are biomolecules composed primarily of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.
  • They include monosaccharides, oligosaccharides and polysaccharides.
  • Glucose is an important aldohexose, while fructose is a ketohexose.
  • Carbohydrate structures exhibit stereoisomerism, including D/L configurations, epimers and anomers.
  • Glycosidic bonds connect monosaccharide units.
  • Starch and glycogen are storage polysaccharides, while cellulose is structural.
  • Carbohydrates contribute to energy production, cellular structure and molecular recognition.
  • Digestion converts digestible carbohydrates into absorbable monosaccharides.
  • Glycolysis, glycogenesis, glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis are important metabolic pathways.
  • Carbohydrate analysis uses chemical tests and instrumental methods.
